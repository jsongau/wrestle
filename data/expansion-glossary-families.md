# SECTION 1 — WRESTLING GLOSSARY

67 terms every fan searches, each written as a standalone answer.

---

### Kayfabe
**slug:** `kayfabe`
Kayfabe is pro wrestling's shared fiction — the industry-wide agreement to present staged rivalries, characters, and outcomes as if they were real and unscripted. For most of the 20th century wrestlers protected kayfabe at all costs, even staying "in character" in public; today it survives as a knowing pact between performers and fans.

### Work
**slug:** `work`
A "work" is anything in wrestling that is planned, scripted, or staged to look real — the opposite of a shoot. Calling something "a work" means it was deliberately staged for the audience, whether a match finish, an injury angle, or a backstage "leak."

### Shoot
**slug:** `shoot`
A "shoot" is something real and unscripted that happens in wrestling — genuine words, a real punch, or an unplanned moment breaking through the scripted show. The term comes from carnival and catch-wrestling slang for a legitimate, non-cooperative contest.

### Worked shoot
**slug:** `worked-shoot`
A worked shoot is a scripted segment deliberately made to look like a real, unscripted shoot, blurring the line between fiction and reality. CM Punk's 2011 "Pipe Bomb" promo (see MAT's CM Punk profile) is the most famous example — planned by WWE yet delivered to feel like Punk had gone off-script.

### Heel
**slug:** `heel`
A heel is a villain — the "bad guy" a wrestler plays to draw boos and give the hero someone to overcome. Great heels cheat, cut arrogant promos, and generate "heat," making the babyface's eventual comeback more satisfying.

### Face (babyface)
**slug:** `face-babyface`
A face, or babyface, is the hero — the good-guy character the crowd is meant to cheer. The role dates to carnival wrestling and remains one half of wrestling's core good-versus-evil dynamic opposite the heel.

### Tweener
**slug:** `tweener`
A tweener is a wrestler who sits between hero and villain — cheered by some fans and booed by others, or morally ambiguous by design. Stone Cold Steve Austin's late-1990s rise (see MAT's Stone Cold profile) is the classic tweener-to-antihero example: a rule-breaker fans loved anyway.

### Turn
**slug:** `turn`
A turn is when a wrestler switches alignment — a face becoming a heel (heel turn) or a heel becoming a face (face turn). A well-executed turn can redefine a career; a shocking heel turn, like Hulk Hogan's at Bash at the Beach 1996 (a MAT-profiled moment), can reshape an entire era.

### Angle
**slug:** `angle`
An angle is a storyline or plot — the scripted narrative that gives matches emotional stakes, from a betrayal to a contract dispute. Angles connect one match to the next and are the engine of long-form wrestling television.

### Gimmick
**slug:** `gimmick`
A gimmick is a wrestler's on-screen character, persona, or hook — the personality, look, and backstory that make them memorable. The Undertaker's supernatural mortician (see MAT's Undertaker profile) is one of wrestling's most enduring gimmicks.

### Promo
**slug:** `promo`
A promo is an interview or in-ring speech a wrestler delivers to advance a storyline, hype a match, or define their character. A strong promo can make a star; The Rock and Stone Cold (both MAT profiles) built their legends largely on the microphone.

### Pipe bomb
**slug:** `pipe-bomb`
"Pipe bomb" refers to a promo that feels dangerously real and shatters kayfabe, named for CM Punk's June 27, 2011 worked-shoot on WWE Raw. Punk sat at the stage edge and aired "real" grievances about the company, launching one of the most influential promos of the modern era (see MAT's CM Punk profile).

### Jobber
**slug:** `jobber`
A jobber is a wrestler whose role is to lose — to "do the job" and make an opponent look strong. Also called "enhancement talent," jobbers were a staple of 1980s–90s TV, taking squash losses to elevate rising stars.

### Put over
**slug:** `put-over`
To "put someone over" is to lose to them, or praise them, in a way that boosts their credibility and status. Veterans putting over younger talent is how the business passes the torch across generations.

### Push
**slug:** `push`
A push is a concerted effort by a promotion to elevate a wrestler through wins, TV time, and big storylines. A "strong push" signals management sees the performer as a future star; a stalled push often frustrates fans.

### Bury
**slug:** `bury`
To "bury" a wrestler is to book them to lose or look weak, damaging their credibility and standing with fans — the opposite of a push. Burying can be a deliberate punishment, a creative misstep, or a byproduct of a rival being pushed at their expense.

### Over
**slug:** `over`
Being "over" means a wrestler has connected with the audience and draws a strong, consistent reaction — cheers for a face, boos for a heel. Getting over is the fundamental goal of any performer; a wrestler who is "super over" can carry a whole promotion.

### Heat
**slug:** `heat`
Heat is negative crowd reaction — the boos, jeers, and hostility a heel works to generate. In the business "good heat" means fans hate the character enough to pay to see them lose; backstage, "heat" can also mean real-life tension between people.

### Cheap heat
**slug:** `cheap-heat`
Cheap heat is booing a heel earns through easy shortcuts — insulting the host city's sports team, the local crowd, or current events — rather than through storyline work. It reliably draws boos but is considered lazy compared to heat built through character and booking.

### Pop
**slug:** `pop`
A pop is a sudden, loud burst of cheering from the crowd, usually for a hero's entrance, a big move, or a surprise return. A "huge pop" is a key measure of how over a moment or wrestler is.

### Mark
**slug:** `mark`
A mark is a fan who takes wrestling at face value, emotionally invested as if it were entirely real — a term borrowed from carnival slang for an easy target. It can be neutral or mildly dismissive; a "smart mark" (smark) is the self-aware counterpart.

### Smark
**slug:** `smark`
A smark (smart mark) is a knowledgeable fan who understands wrestling's inner workings — booking, backstage politics, and industry terms — but still enjoys it. Smarks follow dirt sheets, debate workrate, and often chant to show they're "in the know."

### Spot
**slug:** `spot`
A spot is a planned, discrete moment or sequence of moves within a match — a specific highlight the wrestlers set up and execute. Matches are built as a series of spots woven together by selling and pacing.

### Spotfest
**slug:** `spotfest`
A spotfest is a match packed with flashy, high-impact spots and big moves at the expense of storytelling or psychology. The term can be praise (an exciting, action-dense match) or criticism (moves with no meaning between them).

### Botch
**slug:** `botch`
A botch is a mistake in the execution of a move, spot, or promo — a slip that breaks the illusion or endangers a wrestler. Some botches are harmless and forgotten; others cause serious injury or become infamous clips.

### Sell / No-sell
**slug:** `sell-no-sell`
To "sell" is to react convincingly to an opponent's move as if it hurt; to "no-sell" is to ignore a move and show no effect. Selling makes offense look devastating, while deliberate no-selling can be a storytelling tool for an unstoppable character — or a sign of disrespect.

### Selling
**slug:** `selling`
Selling is the art of reacting to moves, pain, and fatigue to make a match feel real and dramatic. Elite sellers like Shawn Michaels and Ricky Steamboat could make an entire arena believe a near-fall was the end; strong selling is central to wrestling psychology.

### False finish
**slug:** `false-finish`
A false finish is a dramatic near-fall or apparent match-ending moment that the losing wrestler survives, tricking the crowd into thinking the bout is over. Well-timed false finishes are the backbone of great big-match drama.

### Near-fall
**slug:** `near-fall`
A near-fall is a pin attempt broken up an instant before the three-count, at "two-and-a-half." Near-falls create the peak tension of a match and are the mechanism behind most false finishes.

### Kick-out
**slug:** `kick-out`
A kick-out is when a wrestler raises a shoulder to break a pin before the referee's three-count. Dramatic kick-outs from finishers are among the loudest crowd moments in a match.

### Finisher
**slug:** `finisher`
A finisher is a wrestler's signature match-ending move, the one fans expect to score the decisive pin or submission. Iconic finishers — Austin's Stone Cold Stunner, The Rock's Rock Bottom, Undertaker's Tombstone (all MAT profiles) — become shorthand for the performer.

### Signature move
**slug:** `signature-move`
A signature move is a move strongly associated with a wrestler that isn't necessarily their match-ender — a recognizable staple that pops the crowd. It sits a tier below the finisher and helps define a wrestler's in-ring identity.

### Submission
**slug:** `submission`
A submission is a hold applied to force an opponent to "tap out" or verbally quit, ending the match by surrender. Submission specialists like Bret Hart (Sharpshooter) and Kurt Angle (Ankle Lock) — both MAT profiles — built their offense around finishing holds.

### Count-out
**slug:** `count-out`
A count-out occurs when a wrestler stays outside the ring past the referee's count (usually ten), losing the match without being pinned. Count-out finishes protect a wrestler from a clean loss while still advancing a feud.

### DQ (disqualification)
**slug:** `dq-disqualification`
A DQ ends a match when a wrestler breaks the rules badly enough for the referee to award victory to the opponent — using a weapon, refusing to release an illegal hold, or interference. Like count-outs, DQ finishes let a wrestler lose the match without being cleanly beaten, since titles typically don't change hands on a DQ.

### Screwjob
**slug:** `screwjob`
A screwjob is a match finish decided by outside interference, referee shenanigans, or behind-the-scenes manipulation rather than a clean win. The term became infamous after the 1997 Montreal Screwjob (see that entry and MAT's Bret Hart profile).

### Run-in
**slug:** `run-in`
A run-in is when a wrestler not in the match rushes to ringside to interfere, changing the outcome or setting up a new angle. Run-ins are a staple tool for triggering turns, debuts, and screwjob finishes.

### Swerve
**slug:** `swerve`
A swerve is an unexpected twist in a storyline or match designed to shock the audience — a surprise turn, betrayal, or reversal of expectations. Overused swerves can frustrate fans, but a well-earned one, like Seth Rollins cashing in at WrestleMania 31 (a MAT match), becomes legendary.

### Stable / Faction
**slug:** `stable-faction`
A stable, or faction, is a group of wrestlers allied on-screen for mutual benefit, usually against a common enemy. Legendary factions include the nWo, D-Generation X, Evolution, and The Shield (Roman Reigns, Seth Rollins, Jon Moxley — all MAT profiles).

### Tag team
**slug:** `tag-team`
A tag team is two (or more) wrestlers who compete together as a unit, tagging in and out during matches. Great teams — the Hardy Boyz, the Dudley Boyz (Bully Ray, a MAT profile), the Usos — develop their own identities, championships, and rivalries.

### Manager
**slug:** `manager`
A manager is an on-screen character who accompanies a wrestler, handling promos, interference, and psychological warfare on their behalf. Legendary managers like Bobby "The Brain" Heenan and Paul Heyman (Brock Lesnar's advocate, a MAT connection) can make a client feel like a bigger star.

### Valet
**slug:** `valet`
A valet is an on-screen companion, often a glamorous escort, who accompanies a wrestler to the ring and adds to their presentation. The role can overlap with manager duties and has launched many performers into wrestling careers of their own.

### Gimmick match
**slug:** `gimmick-match`
A gimmick match is any bout with special rules or a stipulation beyond standard competition — cage, ladder, hardcore, and more. Gimmick matches raise stakes, blow off long feuds, and create spectacle.

### Hardcore
**slug:** `hardcore`
Hardcore wrestling is a no-disqualification, weapons-heavy style built on extreme violence, popularized in the U.S. by ECW in the 1990s. Hardcore icons like Mick Foley (a MAT profile) built their legends on tables, thumbtacks, and barbed wire.

### Ladder match
**slug:** `ladder-match`
A ladder match is a bout with no pinfalls where wrestlers must climb a ladder to retrieve an object — usually a title belt or contract — suspended above the ring. The stipulation produces some of wrestling's most spectacular and dangerous spots.

### TLC (Tables, Ladders & Chairs)
**slug:** `tlc-tables-ladders-chairs`
A TLC match is a ladder match that legally incorporates tables and chairs as weapons, escalating the carnage. The Hardy Boyz, Dudley Boyz, and Edge & Christian (Edge is a MAT profile) pioneered the format, including the famous TLC II at WrestleMania X-Seven (a MAT match).

### Hell in a Cell
**slug:** `hell-in-a-cell`
Hell in a Cell is a match fought inside a roofed steel cage that fully encloses the ring and ringside area, designed to be a feud's brutal, inescapable conclusion. The 1998 Undertaker–Mankind Cell (MAT's Undertaker and Mick Foley profiles) set the standard for the match's mythology.

### Money in the Bank
**slug:** `money-in-the-bank`
Money in the Bank is a multi-competitor ladder match whose winner earns a contract guaranteeing a world title shot at any time within a year. The unpredictable "cash-in" — like Seth Rollins converting his at WrestleMania 31 (a MAT match) — is one of modern wrestling's signature devices.

### Royal Rumble
**slug:** `royal-rumble`
The Royal Rumble is a 30-competitor over-the-top-rope elimination match where entrants arrive at timed intervals and the last wrestler remaining wins — traditionally earning a world title shot at WrestleMania. It is one of WWE's four biggest annual events.

### Iron Man match
**slug:** `iron-man-match`
An Iron Man match awards victory to whoever scores the most falls (pins, submissions, or other decisions) within a set time limit, usually 30 or 60 minutes. It's an endurance showcase reserved for elite workers, such as the Bret Hart–Shawn Michaels 60-minute classic at WrestleMania XII (both MAT profiles).

### Cage match
**slug:** `cage-match`
A steel cage match surrounds the ring with walls that competitors typically must escape, or win by pin or submission, with the cage preventing interference and escape. It's one of wrestling's oldest gimmick matches and a common way to end a heated feud.

### Squash
**slug:** `squash`
A squash is a one-sided match in which a pushed star quickly and decisively defeats a jobber to look dominant. Squash matches were a defining feature of 1980s–90s TV, establishing new stars' credibility fast.

### Main event
**slug:** `main-event`
The main event is the featured, top-billed match of a card — the marquee attraction fans pay to see. A "main-eventer" is a wrestler established at the top of the card, the highest tier of the roster.

### Mid-card
**slug:** `mid-card`
The mid-card is the tier of the roster and show between opening matches and the main event, often built around secondary titles like the Intercontinental or United States Championship. Strong mid-card work is the proving ground for future main-eventers.

### Card
**slug:** `card`
A card is the full lineup of matches scheduled for a wrestling show, listed in running order from opener to main event. "Carding" a match means booking it onto the show.

### Dark match
**slug:** `dark-match`
A dark match is a bout not televised or streamed — held before or after the broadcast, often to warm up the crowd or audition new talent. Dark matches let wrestlers gain experience away from cameras.

### House show
**slug:** `house-show`
A house show (or live event) is a non-televised event promotions run for ticket revenue, featuring matches that usually don't affect storylines. House shows are where wrestlers refine matches and connect with local crowds.

### PLE / PPV
**slug:** `ple-ppv`
A PPV (pay-per-view) is a premium special event fans historically paid extra to watch; in the streaming era WWE rebranded these as PLEs (Premium Live Events). They are the marquee shows where major feuds and title changes pay off.

### Dirt sheet
**slug:** `dirt-sheet`
A dirt sheet is a wrestling news outlet or newsletter reporting backstage news, rumors, and behind-the-scenes information — the term originated with printed insider newsletters. Modern dirt sheets shape smark discourse and often break real news before promotions confirm it.

### Five-star match
**slug:** `five-star-match`
A five-star match is a bout judged near-perfect on the star-rating scale popularized by journalist Dave Meltzer of the Wrestling Observer. The rating became wrestling's most cited quality benchmark, and Meltzer has since awarded ratings beyond five stars for exceptional matches.

### Draw
**slug:** `draw`
A "draw" is a wrestler or attraction whose star power sells tickets, subscriptions, and pay-per-views. Being "a draw" is the ultimate business measure of value; the term also separately means a match that ends with no winner.

### Gate
**slug:** `gate`
The gate is the total ticket revenue a live event earns from attendance. A record-setting gate signals a major, in-demand show and is a key metric of a card's commercial success.

### Bump
**slug:** `bump`
A bump is the act of falling to the mat or floor to absorb and sell a move safely. Learning to bump — landing to spread impact and protect the body — is one of the first fundamentals a wrestler must master.

### High spot
**slug:** `high-spot`
A high spot is a match's biggest, most spectacular, and often most dangerous moment — a top-rope dive, a ladder plunge, a huge aerial move. High spots are built up to as a match's climactic highlights.

### False comeback
**slug:** `false-comeback`
A false comeback is a brief rally by the wrestler who's losing that raises the crowd's hope before the dominant opponent cuts it off and resumes control. It's a pacing tool that heightens sympathy for the babyface before the real comeback lands.

### Dusty finish
**slug:** `dusty-finish`
A Dusty finish is a match ending where a face appears to win, only for the decision to be reversed — typically on a technicality like a disqualification or a second referee overruling the first. Named for booker Dusty Rhodes (see the Rhodes family), who used it heavily in the NWA to keep feuds hot without decisive title changes.

### Montreal Screwjob (term)
**slug:** `montreal-screwjob`
The Montreal Screwjob is the real, unscripted event at Survivor Series on November 9, 1997, where WWF owner Vince McMahon secretly ordered the referee to ring the bell on Bret Hart, awarding the title to Shawn Michaels against Hart's wishes as Hart was leaving for WCW. It is wrestling's most famous shoot, blurring the line between work and reality, and it directly birthed McMahon's "Mr. McMahon" heel character (see MAT's Bret Hart, Shawn Michaels, and Bret-vs-HBK-Montreal profiles).

## The Anoa'i Family
**slug:** `anoai-family`
**MAT wrestlers involved:** Roman Reigns, The Rock (linked by Samoan tradition)

The Anoa'i family is the most prolific dynasty in wrestling history — a Samoan-American clan spanning four-plus generations. Its roots trace to high chief Amituana'i Anoa'i, and the family expanded through the "Wild Samoans" Afa and Sika Anoa'i, who trained countless relatives. **Roman Reigns** (a MAT profile) is the son of Sika; his ascent to WWE's top star continues the family's dominance. The Anoa'is are also bound by Samoan tradition to the Maivia line: high chief Peter Maivia was considered a blood brother of the family, which makes his grandson **The Rock** (Dwayne Johnson, a MAT profile) part of the extended dynasty even without direct blood ties. Other members include Yokozuna (Rodney Anoa'i), Rikishi (Solofa Fatu), Umaga (Eddie Fatu), Manu, and the modern generation of the Usos, Solo Sikoa, and Jacob Fatu. **Legacy:** no family has placed more members in wrestling, and the Anoa'i dynasty underpins the modern "Bloodline" saga that has anchored WWE for years.

## The Hart Family
**slug:** `hart-family`
**MAT wrestlers involved:** Bret Hart

Founded by patriarch Stu Hart — promoter of Calgary's Stampede Wrestling and operator of the infamous basement training school "the Dungeon" — the Harts are wrestling's foremost technical dynasty. Stu and Helen Hart had twelve children, including **Bret "The Hitman" Hart** (a MAT profile), a five-time WWF Champion and one of the greatest technical wrestlers ever, and Owen Hart, a beloved star whose 1999 in-ring accident remains one of wrestling's darkest days. The family extended through marriage to Davey Boy Smith (the British Bulldog) and Jim "The Anvil" Neidhart, Bret's partner in the Hart Foundation tag team. The next generation includes Natalya (Neidhart's daughter and a longtime WWE women's standout), Harry Smith (Davey Boy's son), and Teddy Hart. **Legacy:** synonymous with mat-based technical excellence, the Harts shaped Canadian wrestling and produced a lineage that still competes in WWE. See MAT's Bret-vs-HBK-Montreal profile for the family's most historic flashpoint.

## The Guerrero Family
**slug:** `guerrero-family`
**MAT wrestlers involved:** Eddie Guerrero

The Guerreros are Mexican-American wrestling royalty, founded by lucha libre legend Gory Guerrero, a pioneer who invented moves still used today (the "Gory Special" bears his name) and was a major star in Mexico's EMLL. His sons Chavo Sr., Mando, Hector, and **Eddie Guerrero** all became wrestlers. Eddie (a MAT profile) grew into a global icon — a masterful "Latino Heat" whose 2004 WWE Championship win was an emotional peak of his generation before his death in 2005 at age 38. The dynasty continues through Chavo Guerrero Jr., Eddie's nephew and frequent on-screen partner, and the family's influence carries into modern lucha and Hollywood (Chavo Jr. choreographed wrestling for GLOW). **Legacy:** the Guerreros bridged Mexican lucha libre and American wrestling, and Eddie's "lie, cheat, and steal" charisma made him one of the most beloved figures in the sport. See MAT's Eddie-vs-Rey rivalry and the Rey Mysterio profile for key connections.

## The Rhodes Family
**slug:** `rhodes-family`
**MAT wrestlers involved:** Cody Rhodes, and (as booker) the "Dusty finish" term

The Rhodes family is American wrestling aristocracy built on "The American Dream" Dusty Rhodes — a three-time NWA World Champion, legendary promo artist, and influential booker whose everyman charisma made him a working-class hero. His sons carry the legacy: Dustin Rhodes, best known for the avant-garde "Goldust" character, and **Cody Rhodes** (a MAT profile), who left WWE, co-founded AEW, then returned to WWE and finally "finished the story" by winning the world title at WrestleMania. Dusty's booking innovations gave the business the "Dusty finish" (see glossary). **Legacy:** three generations of main-event presence and creative influence, from Dusty's booking of the NWA to Cody's role in reshaping the modern wrestling landscape. See MAT's Cody Rhodes profile for detail.

## The Flair Family
**slug:** `flair-family`
**MAT wrestlers involved:** Ric Flair, Charlotte Flair

Headed by "The Nature Boy" Ric Flair — a 16-time world champion widely regarded as one of the greatest of all time — the Flairs blend legendary status with genuine tragedy. **Ric Flair** (a MAT profile) set the template for the flamboyant, limousine-riding heel champion. His son David Flair wrestled in WCW; his son Reid Flair, a promising amateur and pro, died in 2013. His daughter **Charlotte Flair** (Ashley Fliehr, a MAT profile) became a multi-time WWE women's champion and one of the defining performers of the women's division, headlining the first women's WrestleMania main event. **Legacy:** the Flairs pair one of wrestling's most decorated careers with a second-generation star in Charlotte who has arguably matched her father's championship dominance. See MAT's Charlotte Flair profile and the Rousey-Charlotte-Becky WrestleMania 35 match.

## The Von Erich Family
**slug:** `von-erich-family`
**MAT wrestlers involved:** none directly profiled (foundational dynasty)

The Von Erichs were the heart of Texas's World Class Championship Wrestling and the sport's most tragic dynasty. Patriarch Fritz Von Erich (real name Jack Adkisson) was a star and promoter who built the family into 1980s icons across the Dallas–Fort Worth territory. His sons — Kevin, David, Kerry, Mike, and Chris — were beloved regional heroes, with Kerry Von Erich reaching national fame as a WWF Intercontinental Champion. A devastating string of early deaths claimed David, Mike, Chris, and Kerry, leaving Kevin as the surviving brother; their story was dramatized in the 2023 film *The Iron Claw*. The family was inducted into the WWE Hall of Fame in 2009, and Kevin's sons Marshall and Ross have carried the name into a new generation. **Legacy:** the Von Erichs defined the territory era's family-driven star power and remain wrestling's most poignant cautionary tale.

## The McMahon Family
**slug:** `mcmahon-family`
**MAT wrestlers involved:** Triple H (by marriage)

The McMahons are wrestling's ruling business dynasty, spanning four generations of promoters. Jess McMahon promoted boxing and wrestling in the early 20th century; his son Vincent J. McMahon ran the Northeast's WWWF/WWF; and his grandson Vincent K. (Vince) McMahon transformed WWF/WWE into a global entertainment empire and, after the Montreal Screwjob, became the on-screen villain "Mr. McMahon." Vince's children Shane and Stephanie McMahon both became prominent on-screen and executive figures. **Triple H** (Paul Levesque, a MAT profile) married Stephanie McMahon, joining the family both personally and as WWE's Chief Content Officer. **Legacy:** the McMahons built and still shape the largest wrestling company in the world, and their real-life family drama has fueled decades of storylines. See MAT's Triple H profile and Austin-vs-McMahon rivalry — the feud that defined the Attitude Era.

## The Windham / Rotunda Family (Wyatt)
**slug:** `windham-rotunda-family`
**MAT wrestlers involved:** none directly profiled (Bray Wyatt lineage)

This deeply intertwined dynasty links three wrestling names — Mulligan, Windham, and Rotunda — across generations. Patriarch Blackjack Mulligan (Robert Windham) was a towering star of the 1970s–80s; his sons Barry Windham (a Four Horsemen member and NWA World Champion) and Kendall Windham followed him into the ring. Mulligan's daughter married Mike Rotunda (a 1980s–90s star known as "Irwin R. Schyster"/IRS), and their sons became modern stars: Windham Rotunda, better known as **Bray Wyatt** — the cult-leader "Eater of Worlds" and later "The Fiend," a WWE and Universal Champion who died in 2023 at age 36 — and Taylor Rotunda (Bo Dallas / Uncle Howdy). **Legacy:** a third- and fourth-generation dynasty that produced one of the most creative characters of the modern era in Bray Wyatt, whose innovative "Firefly Fun House" and Fiend work redefined wrestling presentation.

## The Orton Family
**slug:** `orton-family`
**MAT wrestlers involved:** Randy Orton

The Ortons are a proud third-generation wrestling family. It began with Bob Orton Sr., a veteran of the territory era; continued through "Cowboy" Bob Orton Jr., a rugged 1980s star famous as Roddy Piper's cast-wearing enforcer; and reached its peak with **Randy Orton** (a MAT profile), the self-styled "Legend Killer" and "Viper." Randy became a multi-time WWE and World Champion, one of the defining main-eventers of his generation, and a member of the Evolution stable alongside Triple H, Ric Flair, and Batista (all MAT profiles). Bob Orton Jr.'s brother Barry Orton also wrestled. **Legacy:** three generations in the business and a marquee modern superstar in Randy, whose longevity and RKO finisher made him a household name. See MAT's Randy Orton profile.

## The Steiner Family
**slug:** `steiner-family`
**MAT wrestlers involved:** none directly profiled (foundational tag dynasty)

The Steiners are an elite athletic wrestling family from Bay City, Michigan, both standout amateur wrestlers at the University of Michigan before turning pro. Rick Steiner (Robert Rechsteiner) and Scott Steiner (Scott Rechsteiner) formed the Steiner Brothers, one of the most decorated and physically dominant tag teams ever, winning championships across WWF, WCW, Japan, and the NWA with a suplex-heavy, hard-hitting style. Scott later reinvented himself as the outrageous "Big Poppa Pump" singles star. The next generation arrived with Rick's son Bron Breakker (Bronson Rechsteiner), a former college football player turned explosive WWE star and multi-time NXT Champion. **Legacy:** the Steiners set a standard for athletic, amateur-rooted tag-team wrestling, and Bron Breakker carries that intensity into WWE's current era.

## The Briscoe Family
**slug:** `briscoe-family`
**MAT wrestlers involved:** none directly profiled (Ring of Honor cornerstone)

The Briscoe Brothers — Jay Briscoe (Jamin Pugh) and Mark Briscoe (Mark Pugh) — are a real-life sibling duo from Delaware who became the foundational tag team of Ring of Honor. Debuting as teenagers, "Dem Boys" won the ROH World Tag Team Championship a record number of times over two decades, defining the promotion with their intense, hard-hitting, redneck-brawler style and unmatched chemistry. Jay was also a two-time ROH World Champion. The team's story turned tragic when Jay Briscoe died in a car crash in January 2023; Mark has since continued as a singles competitor in AEW/ROH, honoring his brother. **Legacy:** the most important tag team in Ring of Honor history and a symbol of independent-wrestling loyalty and grit.

## The Samoan / Uso Lineage (Modern Bloodline)
**slug:** `samoan-uso-lineage`
**MAT wrestlers involved:** Roman Reigns, The Rock

A modern branch of the Anoa'i dynasty, this lineage centers on Rikishi (Solofa Fatu) and his sons, and forms the backbone of WWE's "Bloodline" storyline. Rikishi — a former WWF star famous for the "Stinkface" and later a WWE Hall of Famer — is the father of twins Jey and Jimmy Uso (the most decorated tag team in WWE history) and Solo Sikoa. On television these cousins rallied around **Roman Reigns** (a MAT profile) as "The Tribal Chief," creating the era-defining Bloodline faction, later joined and challenged by **The Rock** (a MAT profile) in a real-life family collision. The lineage also connects to Umaga (Eddie Fatu, Rikishi's cousin), the "Samoan Werewolf" Jacob Fatu, and — through Jimmy Snuka's marriage into the family — Tamina and the extended Snuka line. **Legacy:** the Uso/Samoan branch turned decades of family history into WWE's most acclaimed long-form storyline, making the Bloodline the emotional core of modern WWE. See MAT's Roman Reigns profile and The-Bloodline rivalry.
